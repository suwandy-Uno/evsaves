What Does It Really Cost to Run an EV in Australia?

The true running cost of an EV in Australia goes beyond electricity. When you add up all the costs — charging, insurance, registration, servicing, tyres, and roadside assistance — you get the complete picture of what your electric vehicle actually costs to operate each year. For a typical Australian driving 15,000 km per year, the total running cost of an EV is significantly lower than an equivalent petrol car.

Electricity (charging): At the national average home charging rate of 32c/kWh, and assuming a typical EV efficiency of around 16 kWh/100km, annual charging costs for 15,000 km work out to approximately $768 per year. Drivers on time-of-use plans or with solar panels can bring this down to $300–$500 per year.

Insurance: Comprehensive car insurance for an EV in Australia typically runs between $1,100 and $1,800 per year, depending on the make, model, driver profile, and state. According to data from iSelect and Compare the Market, EV insurance premiums have moderated significantly since 2023 as insurers have gained more experience with EV repair and parts costs. CTP (Compulsory Third Party) insurance is a separate cost, typically $300–$600/year depending on your state and vehicle rating. NSW drivers pay CTP separately to registration; other states bundle it into rego fees.

Registration: Annual vehicle registration costs in Australia vary by state and vehicle weight/engine size. For a typical passenger EV, expect to pay approximately $300–$700 per year. Some states previously offered stamp duty exemptions that reduced upfront costs; ongoing registration rates are broadly similar to petrol equivalents. Victoria, NSW, ACT, and Queensland have various EV incentives that may affect first-year costs.

Servicing: This is where EVs shine compared to petrol cars. Without an engine, transmission, exhaust system, or coolant system to service, EV maintenance is dramatically simpler. RACQ data from their vehicle running cost studies shows that EV servicing averages around $250–$400 per year for most models — compared to $600–$1,200 for comparable petrol cars. NRMA and RAA publish similar figures. A typical EV annual service involves an inspection, cabin air filter, wiper blades, tyre rotation, and brake fluid check. Brake wear is also reduced by regenerative braking, which handles most low-speed deceleration without touching the friction brakes.

Tyres: EVs are generally heavier than equivalent petrol cars due to battery weight, which can increase tyre wear slightly. Budget around $600–$900 per set of four tyres for a mainstream EV, with a replacement interval of roughly 30,000–45,000 km depending on driving style. This works out to approximately $200–$300 per year for a 15,000 km driver.

Roadside assistance: RACQ, NRMA, RAA, and RAC all offer EV-compatible roadside assistance. Annual membership costs around $150–$250 depending on the level of cover and your state. All major motoring clubs now have mobile charging units capable of providing enough charge to get you to the nearest public charger if you run flat.

Adding it all up, a typical EV driver in Australia paying average electricity rates can expect total running costs of around $3,500–$5,000 per year for 15,000 km — compared to $6,000–$9,000 for a comparable petrol car once you include fuel, servicing, and all other costs. Frequently Asked Questions

Annual EV servicing in Australia typically costs $250–$400, covering inspection, cabin air filter, wiper blades, brake fluid check, and tyre rotation. This compares favourably to $600–$1,200 for a comparable petrol car. RACQ, NRMA and RAA all publish vehicle running cost studies confirming EVs have substantially lower servicing requirements. Some models like the Tesla Model 3 have no fixed service intervals and only require attention when the vehicle logs an issue.
EV insurance premiums have been higher than petrol equivalents in the past due to higher repair costs and imported parts. However, as more insurers enter the market and parts availability improves, premiums have become more competitive. In 2026, comprehensive insurance for a mainstream EV like the MG4 or BYD Atto 3 typically costs $1,100–$1,400/year — comparable to an equivalent petrol SUV. Premium EVs (Tesla, Polestar) attract higher premiums of $1,500–$2,000+.
Electricity cost alone works out to approximately 5–6 cents per km at home charging rates (32c/kWh). When you include all running costs — electricity, insurance, rego, servicing, and tyres — the total comes to roughly 23–35 cents per km depending on the vehicle and your electricity rate. A comparable petrol car typically costs 40–60 cents per km all-in, making EVs substantially cheaper to run on a per-kilometre basis.
Standard ongoing registration fees are broadly similar to petrol equivalents in most states. However, the upfront stamp duty can be significantly lower or zero for EVs in states like Victoria, ACT, and SA (where rebates apply). Some states also offer free or discounted luxury car tax thresholds for fuel-efficient vehicles. Checking your specific state's transport authority website is recommended as policies continue to evolve.
EVs tend to be heavier due to their battery packs, which can increase tyre wear compared to a comparable petrol car. However, regenerative braking reduces the amount of friction braking, which can offset some tyre wear at the rear. In practice, most EV owners report tyre life comparable to or slightly less than their previous petrol cars. Using EV-rated tyres designed for heavier loads can help maintain tyre longevity. Budget for one set of four tyres every 30,000–45,000 km.
