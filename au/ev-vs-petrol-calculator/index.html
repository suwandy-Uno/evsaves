How Much Do Australians Save Driving Electric vs Petrol?

With petrol prices averaging $1.89 per litre across Australia in 2026 and home electricity sitting around 32 cents per kWh, switching to an EV delivers some of the most compelling fuel savings in the developed world. The typical Australian driver covers around 15,000 km per year, and at those distances, the energy cost difference between a petrol car and an EV is striking.

A conventional petrol car averaging 8.5L/100km will burn through approximately 1,275 litres per year at 15,000 km. At $1.89/L that's over $2,400 in fuel costs annually. A comparable electric vehicle consuming around 16 kWh/100km uses roughly 2,400 kWh per year. Charged at home at 32c/kWh, that's just $768 — a saving of more than $1,600 every single year on fuel alone.

But the picture gets even better when you factor in Australia's strong take-up of rooftop solar. With more than 3.5 million Australian homes now sporting solar panels, many EV owners are effectively charging their cars for close to zero cost during daylight hours. Solar self-consumption (charging at home from your own panels) can slash the effective charging cost to as little as 5–8 cents per kWh, or even zero if you have a large system and a home battery.

State-by-state electricity price variations also matter. New South Wales drivers typically pay 28–36c/kWh, Victorian households pay 26–32c/kWh, Queensland residents enjoy some of the lowest rates at 25–30c/kWh, and Western Australia sits around 30c/kWh under the Synergy tariff. The calculator above uses the national average of 32c/kWh, but you can adjust this to your actual rate for a more personalised result.

It's not just about fuel. EVs have significantly fewer moving parts than internal combustion engine vehicles. No oil changes, no timing belt, no exhaust system repairs — the servicing bill for a typical EV in Australia is roughly half that of an equivalent petrol car. RACQ, NRMA and RAA data consistently shows EV servicing costs 40–50% below equivalent ICE vehicles once you strip out fuel-related maintenance items.

One major factor unique to Australia is the Fringe Benefits Tax (FBT) exemption for eligible electric vehicles used under a novated lease. Since April 2022, battery electric vehicles and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs) priced below the luxury car tax threshold (currently around $89,332 for fuel-efficient vehicles) are exempt from FBT when provided as part of a novated lease arrangement. This can effectively reduce the cost of an EV by 20–45% for employees who salary-sacrifice, depending on their marginal tax rate. For someone earning $120,000 a year, the combined tax saving from FBT exemption and GST input credit can make an EV dramatically cheaper than buying outright.

Australia's luxury car tax (LCT) is another consideration for higher-priced EVs. Vehicles over the $89,332 fuel-efficient LCT threshold attract a 33% tax on the excess, which adds to the purchase price of premium EVs. However, once you're comparing total operating costs over 5 years — fuel, servicing, registration, and depreciation — even premium EVs often come out ahead of their petrol equivalents for high-kilometre drivers.

The EV market in Australia has grown enormously, with electric vehicles now accounting for over 8% of new car sales in 2025. Prices have fallen significantly, with the MG4 available from around $38,990 drive-away, the BYD Atto 3 from $44,990, and the Tesla Model 3 from around $59,900. As supply has improved and competition has intensified, EVs are now within reach of mainstream Australian buyers — and the running cost savings make the financial case compelling for most drivers.

Frequently Asked Questions

At the national average electricity rate of 32c/kWh, charging a typical EV costs around $5.12 per 100km. Filling a petrol car at $1.89/L averaging 8.5L/100km costs around $16.07 per 100km. That's a saving of roughly $11 per 100km — or about $1,650 per year for a 15,000km driver.
The FBT exemption applies to battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs) whose first retail price is below the luxury car tax threshold for fuel-efficient vehicles (around $89,332 in 2025–26). Standard hybrid vehicles (like the Toyota Camry Hybrid) are not eligible. PHEVs were included in the exemption but eligibility is subject to ongoing legislative review — check the ATO website or consult a salary packaging provider for the latest rules.
Petrol prices in Australia are tied to global crude oil prices and the AUD/USD exchange rate. Historically, fuel prices have trended upward over time with periodic dips. Most analysts expect continued volatility. Switching to an EV removes your exposure to petrol price fluctuations entirely, providing greater cost predictability — especially if you charge at home on a fixed-rate electricity plan.
In cities, EV running costs are typically lower because home charging is convenient and public charging networks (Chargefox, NRMA, AmpCharge) are accessible. Regional drivers may rely more heavily on DC fast chargers for longer trips, which cost 50–60c/kWh. However, regional electricity tariffs are often slightly lower, and the longer distances between destinations mean EVs with large battery packs (like the Tesla Model Y Long Range or BYD Atto 3) are the most suitable choices.
Payback period depends on the price premium of the EV over an equivalent petrol car. For budget EVs like the MG4, where the price premium over a comparable petrol hatchback is relatively small, payback from fuel savings alone can be 3–4 years. For premium EVs, payback is typically 5–8 years on fuel savings alone. Factor in FBT exemption through novated leasing and the payback period can drop dramatically — sometimes to under 2 years on a post-tax basis for eligible employees.
