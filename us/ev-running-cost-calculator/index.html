Full Annual EV Running Costs in the USA

The sticker price of an electric vehicle is only one piece of the financial picture. To understand the true value of EV ownership, you need to look at the complete annual running cost — every dollar it takes to keep that vehicle on the road each year. For most US EV owners, this full picture is highly favorable compared to equivalent gas vehicles.

Electricity costs are the dominant variable running expense for EV owners. At the US national average of $0.16/kWh, a typical EV covering 12,000 miles annually at 3.5 miles/kWh spends roughly $549 on electricity. This is the EV's equivalent of a gas budget — and it is roughly one-third of what a typical gas car owner spends on fuel for the same distance at $3.45/gal.

Servicing and maintenance is where EV ownership offers some of its most consistent savings. According to US Department of Energy data, EV owners spend an average of $0.061 per mile on maintenance, compared to $0.101 per mile for ICE vehicles — a savings of about $480 per year on 12,000 miles. There are no oil changes, no spark plugs, no coolant flushes, and no timing belt replacements. A typical annual service for an EV involves tire rotation, brake inspection, and cabin air filter replacement — generally running $200–$400 at a dealership or independent service center.

Insurance is one area where EVs can cost more than equivalent gas vehicles. The AAA reports that EVs average roughly $2,280 per year to insure nationally — about 20% higher than comparable gas vehicles. This premium reflects higher repair costs, expensive battery components, and limited body shop capabilities for EV repairs. However, insurance costs vary enormously by state, driving history, vehicle model, and insurer. Shopping for EV-specific policies from companies experienced with EV claims can reduce premiums substantially.

Tire wear deserves a mention. EVs are significantly heavier than equivalent gas vehicles due to battery weight. A Tesla Model Y weighs approximately 4,400 lbs versus around 3,300 lbs for a comparable Toyota RAV4. This additional weight, combined with the instant torque delivery of electric motors, accelerates tire wear. EV owners typically report needing tire replacement 20–30% sooner than gas car owners — an added cost of $150–$300 per year when spread over a tire lifespan.

Registration fees vary widely by state. Some states like Texas, Illinois, and Wyoming charge EVs additional annual fees ($200–$400) to compensate for gas tax revenue lost to EV drivers who do not pay at the pump. Meanwhile, states like California, Colorado, and Oregon focus more on incentives than penalties. Check your state's DMV for specific EV registration costs in your area.

Total annual running costs range from approximately $2,200 to $4,500 depending on location and vehicle model — often $600–$1,200 less per year than an equivalent gas vehicle.

EV brakes also last significantly longer due to regenerative braking. Consumer Reports survey data consistently shows EV owners report lower repair and maintenance costs than ICE vehicle owners across multiple years of ownership.

Some states are moving toward vehicle miles traveled (VMT) fees as an alternative approach. Free workplace charging can dramatically reduce annual electricity costs. If your employer provides free Level 2 charging and you work 5 days a week, you could charge 10–15 kWh per working day — covering most of your commute energy needs. At 250 working days per year, free workplace charging could save $400–$600 annually. Many large US employers — including Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and numerous hospitals and universities — now offer free or subsidized EV charging as an employee benefit. Always check before assuming this benefit is available.
