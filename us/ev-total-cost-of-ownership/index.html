Is an EV Actually Cheaper Over 5 Years in the USA?

Total cost of ownership (TCO) is the definitive financial measure for comparing EV vs gas vehicle economics. Unlike a simple sticker price comparison, TCO captures every dollar you spend owning and operating a vehicle over a defined period — typically five years. When calculated properly, EVs increasingly come out ahead of their gas equivalents for the majority of US drivers, even when the EV has a higher purchase price.

A landmark Consumer Reports study found that EV owners in the US spend about half as much on repairs and maintenance as gas car owners over the vehicle's lifetime. When combined with lower fuel costs, this results in total ownership cost savings of $6,000 to $10,000 over five years for average US drivers, compared to a similarly-priced gas vehicle. The DOE's Vehicle Cost Calculator and ANL's BioEnergy Analysis corroborate these findings with detailed modeling.

The federal IRA tax credit dramatically changes the purchase price equation. For qualifying EVs, buyers can receive up to $7,500 off the effective purchase price. Since 2024, this credit can be applied directly at the point of sale — you simply hand over $7,500 less to the dealer, regardless of your tax liability. This front-loads a significant financial benefit that materially improves the 5-year TCO calculation versus gas vehicles.

Depreciation is the largest single cost component of vehicle ownership for most Americans. EV depreciation has historically been higher than for equivalent gas vehicles, largely due to battery technology uncertainty and rapid market evolution. However, the picture has diverged significantly by brand. Tesla Model 3 and Model Y retain value exceptionally well — often better than their gas equivalents. Chevy Bolt and Nissan Leaf have historically depreciated faster. The introduction of new battery chemistry and competitive pricing has moderated depreciation for most mainstream EVs by 2026.

Financing costs should also factor into your TCO. Because EVs typically have higher sticker prices, financing costs are higher in absolute dollar terms. However, some states and utilities offer low-interest EV loans. The California Clean Vehicle Assistance Program, for example, offers subsidized financing for lower-income buyers. Compare the full financed cost — not just the monthly payment — when evaluating total ownership.

State incentives add further to the TCO advantage in many markets. Colorado offers $5,000 in state tax credits, California up to $7,500 through its Clean Vehicle Rebate Project (CVAP) for qualifying buyers, and New York provides up to $2,000. When stacked with the $7,500 federal credit, purchase cost differences versus gas vehicles can effectively disappear in incentive-rich states.
