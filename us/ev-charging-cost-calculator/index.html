EV Charging Costs in the USA: Home vs Public Networks

Understanding how much it costs to charge your electric vehicle in the US requires separating two very different charging scenarios: home charging and public charging. The difference in cost per mile between these two options is significant, and knowing how to maximize your home charging while minimizing reliance on expensive public networks is one of the key habits that separates high-savings EV owners from average ones.

Home Level 2 charging is by far the cheapest way to fuel your EV. At the national average residential rate of $0.16 per kWh, charging a vehicle that does 3.5 miles per kWh costs about 4.6 cents per mile. Compare that to gasoline at $3.45/gal in a 28 MPG car, which works out to 12.3 cents per mile — nearly three times more expensive. Most EV owners handle 80–90% of their charging at home, keeping their effective per-mile cost exceptionally low.

Time-of-use (TOU) electricity rates can lower home charging costs even further. Many US utilities — including PG&E, Con Edison, and Georgia Power — offer TOU plans where electricity drops to $0.10–$0.13 per kWh during off-peak hours (typically 9pm–6am). Scheduling your EV to charge overnight takes full advantage of these rates. Some utilities also offer dedicated EV charging rates that are even cheaper than standard off-peak pricing.

Tesla Supercharger rates vary by location but average around $0.25–$0.35 per kWh at most US sites. Some high-demand urban locations charge $0.48/kWh. On a per-mile basis, Supercharging typically costs 7–10 cents per mile — still cheaper than gasoline but more than double home charging costs. Tesla offers a Supercharger subscription plan in some markets that can reduce per-session costs for frequent road trippers.

Electrify America, the largest non-Tesla public DC fast charging network in the US, typically charges $0.43–$0.48 per kWh for non-members, or $0.31–$0.35/kWh on its $4/month membership plan. ChargePoint pricing varies as it is a network of independently-operated stations, typically ranging from $0.20–$0.45/kWh depending on the host. Blink Network rates average $0.39–$0.49/kWh at DCFC stations.

On a pure per-mile basis, smart EV drivers who charge primarily at home and only occasionally use public fast chargers typically achieve a blended charging cost of $0.05–$0.07 per mile — compared to $0.12–$0.16 per mile for most gas vehicles. That difference, multiplied across 12,000 annual miles, represents $840–$1,320 in annual savings from fuel costs alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

At the national average rate of $0.16/kWh, a full charge for a 75 kWh battery (like a Tesla Model 3 Long Range) costs about $12. A larger 100 kWh battery (like a Rivian R1T) costs around $16 for a full charge. Most EV owners do not regularly charge from 0–100%; daily top-ups of 20–30 kWh are typical for average US driving distances, costing $3.20–$4.80 per day at home rates.
Home charging is significantly cheaper. Tesla Supercharger rates average $0.25–$0.35/kWh versus $0.16/kWh at home. A full charge at a Supercharger for a 75 kWh battery costs roughly $19–$26, versus $12 at home. For road trips, Superchargers are convenient and fast, but building your driving routine around home charging keeps your per-mile costs at their lowest. Many Tesla owners keep public charging to under 15% of their total energy usage.
Time-of-use (TOU) rates are electricity pricing structures where utilities charge different rates depending on time of day. During off-peak hours — typically overnight from 9pm to 6am — rates can fall to $0.10–$0.13/kWh, sometimes even lower. For EV owners who schedule overnight charging, TOU plans can cut charging costs by 20–40% compared to flat-rate tariffs. Contact your utility or check their website to see if a TOU or EV-specific rate is available in your area.

Tesla Supercharger rates average $0.25–$0.35/kWh for Tesla vehicles, or $0.37–$0.48/kWh for non-Tesla vehicles using the network (CCS adapter required). Electrify America is generally slightly more expensive but serves all EV brands, while Superchargers historically served only Tesla vehicles with better reliability ratings.

At Electrify America rates of $0.45/kWh, the per-mile cost rises to about $0.13. For comparison, a 28 MPG gas car at $3.45/gal costs $0.123 per mile. Home charging is consistently 2–3x cheaper per mile than gasoline in most US states, making the operational savings case for EV ownership very strong for drivers who can charge at home.
