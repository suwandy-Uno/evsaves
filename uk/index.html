UK EV Frequently Asked Questions

At the 2026 Ofgem price cap rate of approximately 24.5p/kWh, a full charge of a 75kWh battery (e.g. Tesla Model Y Long Range) costs around £18.40. For the average UK driver covering 9,000 miles per year in an EV with a real-world efficiency of about 3.5 miles/kWh, annual home charging costs work out to roughly £630–£680. Use our EV Charging Cost Calculator to get a precise figure for your car.

At current UK prices (134p/litre petrol, 24.5p/kWh home electricity), a driver covering 9,000 miles per year in a 40mpg petrol car spends around £1,360 on fuel. The equivalent EV costs around £650 per year on home electricity — a saving of approximately £710. Higher mileage drivers save proportionally more. Enter your own mileage and current fuel costs in our EV vs Petrol Calculator for an accurate figure.

Yes — from April 2025, EVs registered on or after 1 April 2025 are no longer exempt from Vehicle Excise Duty (VED). New EVs now pay the standard first-year rate and then the flat rate from year two, currently £195/yr. However, EVs still avoid the expensive first-year rates that petrol and diesel cars over 255g/km CO2 pay (up to £2,745), and they are exempt from the premium car supplement on cars under £40,000 MSRP. Use our Road Tax Savings Calculator to see your specific situation.

The Benefit-in-Kind (BIK) rate for fully electric company cars in 2026/27 is 3% of the car's P11D value. This is dramatically lower than petrol or diesel company cars, which attract BIK rates of 20–37% depending on CO2 emissions. A higher-rate taxpayer with a £45,000 EV company car pays approximately £540/year in BIK tax, versus £4,000–£6,600 for an equivalent petrol car. Use our Company Car BIK Calculator for your specific figures.

For most employees with access to a salary sacrifice scheme, yes — it is currently one of the cheapest ways to drive a new EV in the UK. Payments come from pre-tax and pre-NI salary, the employer's NI saving is often passed back to the employee, and the 3% BIK rate means the tax cost is minimal. A basic-rate taxpayer can effectively get a new EV for 30–40% less than the headline lease price. Use our Salary Sacrifice Calculator to see your saving.
