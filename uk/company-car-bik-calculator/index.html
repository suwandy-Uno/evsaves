UK Company Car BIK Tax Explained: Why EVs Are Transformative for Company Car Drivers

Benefit in Kind (BIK) tax on company cars is one of the most consequential financial levers in UK personal taxation — and the current disparity between electric vehicles and petrol cars is creating one of the most compelling financial arguments for EV adoption ever seen in the UK market. If you receive or are offered a company car, understanding BIK is not optional; it's essential.

What is Benefit in Kind (BIK) Tax?

When an employer provides a company car that can be used for personal journeys, HMRC treats the benefit as a form of income. The value of this benefit is calculated as a percentage of the car's P11D value (the list price including options and VAT, minus the first registration fee and road tax). This calculated benefit is then added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate — basic rate (20%), higher rate (40%), or additional rate (45%).

The percentage used to calculate the benefit depends on the car's CO2 emissions. This is where the extraordinary disparity between electric and petrol vehicles emerges.

EV BIK Rates: 2026/27 and Future Years

For the 2026/27 tax year, zero-emission vehicles (EVs) attract a BIK rate of just 3%. This rate will rise gradually in future years: 4% in 2027/28, 5% in 2028/29, and the government has indicated further modest increases thereafter. Even at 5%, EVs will remain dramatically more tax-efficient than petrol equivalents.

For context, petrol-powered company cars attract BIK rates of 25% (for cars emitting around 100–110 g/km CO2), rising to 37% for higher-emission vehicles. Diesel cars attract an additional 4% surcharge. These rates have been frozen or increased incrementally by successive governments, while EV rates were specifically set low to encourage fleet electrification.

A Real-World Example: Tesla Model 3 vs BMW 3 Series

Consider a higher-rate (40%) taxpayer choosing between a Tesla Model 3 Long Range (P11D value: £45,000) and a BMW 330i petrol (P11D value: £42,000):

Tesla Model 3 (3% BIK, 2026/27): £45,000 × 3% = £1,350 taxable benefit. Tax at 40% = £540 per year in company car tax.

BMW 330i petrol (at 33% BIK for ~150 g/km CO2): £42,000 × 33% = £13,860 taxable benefit. Tax at 40% = £5,544 per year in company car tax.

The annual saving is £5,004 — over £400 per month in take-home pay — purely from the difference in BIK rates. For a basic-rate taxpayer the saving is still approximately £2,500 per year. Over a three-year company car cycle, a higher-rate taxpayer would save approximately £15,000 in income tax by choosing the EV.

P11D Value vs List Price: What Gets Taxed

The P11D value used for BIK calculation is the list price including all factory-fitted options, VAT, and delivery charges, but excluding the first registration fee and annual road tax. It is not reduced by any deposit, contribution, or actual price paid — if your employer negotiates a fleet discount, HMRC still uses the full manufacturer's list price for BIK purposes. This means the P11D value typically equals or closely approximates the published retail price of the car with options.

Company Car Fuel Benefit: Avoid if Possible

A separate fuel benefit charge applies if an employer provides free fuel (including electricity) for personal use. For petrol cars, this is based on a fixed multiplier (£27,800 for 2026/27) multiplied by the BIK percentage — making it expensive for all but the highest petrol mileage drivers. For EVs, HMRC's position on employer-funded electricity charging is more nuanced. Charging at work or using employer-provided charge cards for business journeys is generally not treated as a benefit in kind; home charging reimbursements require careful structuring to avoid triggering a benefit.

How Salary Sacrifice Interacts with BIK

Many employers now offer EVs through salary sacrifice schemes, where the lease cost is deducted from gross salary before tax and National Insurance are calculated. This is distinct from a traditional company car BIK arrangement — salary sacrifice reduces your gross income (saving income tax and NI on the sacrifice amount) while you still pay BIK tax on the car. For EVs at 3% BIK, this combination of reduced income tax, NI savings, and low BIK can result in an EV costing a higher-rate taxpayer less in total deductions than renting the equivalent car privately at market rates.
