Understanding EV Charging Costs in the UK

One of the most common questions from people considering an EV is: how much will it actually cost me to charge? The answer depends on three things — your electricity rate, how often you use public chargers, and the efficiency of your specific vehicle. This calculator puts all three together to give you a clear monthly and annual charging cost.

Home charging is the foundation of most EV ownership in the UK. Under the current Ofgem price cap, the standard unit rate for electricity is approximately 24.5p per kWh. For a car like a Tesla Model 3 Long Range with a 75 kWh battery, a full charge from a typical 20% starting point costs around £14.70 at standard rates. Switch to a dedicated EV tariff and the same charge drops dramatically. Octopus Go, one of the most popular EV-specific tariffs in the UK, offers rates as low as 7p per kWh during off-peak hours (typically midnight to 5am). That same charge at 7p/kWh costs just £4.20 — a saving of over £10 per full charge. Over a year with weekly charges, that tariff choice alone saves around £500.

Public charging is a necessary part of longer journeys, but it comes at a steep premium in the UK. Rapid chargers (50 kW) and ultra-rapid chargers (150 kW+) at motorway services and urban charge points typically charge between 65p and 79p per kWh in 2026. Some premium locations exceed 85p/kWh. At 70p/kWh, adding 50 kWh costs £35 — comparable to filling up a small petrol car. The key insight is that EV savings depend heavily on minimising your reliance on public charging.

Charging efficiency matters more than most people realise. When you draw electricity from the wall, some is lost as heat in the charging process before it reaches your battery. Typical AC charging efficiency is 88–92% for home wallboxes, meaning roughly 10% of the electricity you pay for doesn't make it into the battery. Rapid DC charging can be slightly more efficient. Our calculator applies an 88% efficiency factor by default, which you can adjust to match your charger type.

Your pence per mile figure is the most useful metric to understand. A car with a real-world efficiency of 3.5 miles per kWh charged at 24.5p gives a charging cost of about 7p per mile. At the Octopus Go rate of 7p/kWh, that falls to just 2p per mile. Compare that to a 35 MPG petrol car at 134p/litre, which costs around 15.5p per mile for fuel alone. Knowing your pence per mile lets you make a direct, fair comparison with any petrol or diesel alternative.

Frequently Asked Questions

At the Ofgem cap rate of 24.5p/kWh, charging a 60 kWh battery from 20% to 100% costs around £11.76. On a dedicated EV overnight tariff at 7p/kWh, the same charge costs just £3.36. Larger batteries like the 100 kWh pack in a Tesla Model S will cost proportionally more. The battery size and your electricity rate are the two main variables — both of which you can adjust in the calculator above.
Public rapid chargers in the UK typically cost between 65p and 79p per kWh in 2026. Networks including BP Pulse, Osprey, GridServe, and Pod Point charge in this range. Some motorway services chargers exceed 85p/kWh, particularly on older networks. Ionity charges around 69p/kWh for non-subscription users. Always check the network app for current pricing before plugging in, as rates vary by location and time of day.
As of 2026, Octopus Go offers one of the cheapest EV overnight rates at around 7p/kWh between midnight and 5am. OVO Charge Anytime provides similar off-peak rates and works with a wider range of smart chargers. EDF GoElectric and E.ON Next Drive are also worth comparing. Most cheap EV tariffs require a smart meter and a compatible smart home charger. Without these, you pay the standard Ofgem rate.
Not all electricity drawn from the wall goes into your battery. Charging efficiency is typically 85–92% for AC home charging, meaning if you draw 70 kWh from the grid, around 60–65 kWh actually reaches the battery. The remaining energy is lost as heat in the onboard charger and cables. Our calculator accounts for this by applying an 88% efficiency factor by default, so your cost per mile figure reflects real-world usage rather than the official WLTP energy consumption figures.
Home charging is almost always significantly cheaper. At 24.5p/kWh at home versus 70p/kWh at a public rapid charger, you are paying nearly three times as much per kWh in public. Drivers who rely primarily on public charging can expect fuel costs closer to petrol levels, whereas those who charge at home overnight typically see their fuel costs fall by 70–80% compared to petrol. If you live in a flat or cannot install a home charger, factor this into your EV ownership calculations carefully.
